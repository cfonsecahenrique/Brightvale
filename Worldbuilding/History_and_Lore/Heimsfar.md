# Heimsfar

*The Homeward Voyage*

**Heimsfar** is the [[Dwarvenkind|Dwarven]] prophecy of return — the belief, held by every Dwarf in [[Hvergi]] in some form or another, that one day their people will reach open water, build great ships, and sail back to the homeland from which the [[Apostasy]] severed them. It is the most durable single conviction in [[Hvergi]]'s culture, and the most debated.

---

## Origins and Transmission

No one knows the prophecy's original form. The oldest surviving rune-carving in [[Hvergi]] that references it dates to approximately **60 AA** — and that carving is already a copy. Every generation of rune-carvers has worked from a slightly worn original, and with each iteration specifics have degraded into symbol and feeling. What was once detailed has become impressionistic: *ships on dark water*, *fire on the horizon*, *a shore that is not this shore*, *the smell of salt and something older than the [[Apostasy]]*.

The details lost to copying include the name and location of the homeland, the nature of the sea to be crossed, the number of ships required, whether the voyage is a single generation's act or a centuries-long migration, and whether the conditions described in the original were geographic, spiritual, or both. Scholars within [[Hvergi]] who study the rune-sequences are divided on a question that would have seemed absurd to the first carvers: whether the carvings describe a real place that can be found, or whether four centuries of erosion have transformed a navigator's record into something that now only functions as myth. The first carvers knew the answer. That knowledge did not survive them intact.

What has survived undamaged across every version — every faded stone, every uncertain copy — is the conviction itself: The sea is out there. The Dwarves will reach it.

---

## The Great Sea

The [[Dwarvenkind|Dwarves]] arrived on the Armisian coastline less than a century before the [[Apostasy]] — close enough that the crossing was living memory for the first generation to settle. They knew where they had come from. The earliest rune-carvings of *Heimsfar* were not myth. They were records: coastal landmarks, sea-routes, estimated distances, the names of the headlands they had left behind. Precise, practical, intended to be used.

What happened to that precision is the quiet tragedy at the heart of [[Hvergi]]'s culture. Five hundred years of re-carving — each generation copying from a slightly worn original, each copy introducing small errors, elisions, guesses — have reduced specific sailing directions to impressionistic symbol. 

The [[Apostasy]] and the march north with the Dawnfather severed the Dwarves from any coast entirely. Brightvale is landlocked. For Hvergi's inhabitants, *Heimsfar* is therefore the longest possible expedition: not into the Wastes below, but through them — past the horizon the [[Explorers' Guild]] has not yet mapped, to wherever the mountains finally end and the water begins again. The directions were once known. The task now is to find them again.

---

## How It Is Carried

*Heimsfar* moves differently through the three ruling families of [[Hvergi]].

**[[Clan Brumdain]]** carries it as literal mission. Elder Storvald Brumdain's secret northeastern tunnel is understood within the family as the first practical step of the prophecy — the edge of the Wastes found, the direction confirmed, the voyage made possible. Storvald does not quote the degraded rune-texts. He acts as though the direction is clear, even where the words are not.

**[[Clan Hjalrim]]** carries it as martial obligation. The voyage requires a people capable of surviving long enough to reach the water. Every blade forged and every gate held is, to the Hjalrims, a verse of *Heimsfar* kept alive in practice rather than recited from stone.

**[[Clan Sigr]]** carries it with the most ambivalence. They believe in it — every Dwarf does — but they are the family most aware of how much has been lost in transmission, and the least willing to act on an interpolation as though it were original text. They argue that if the voyage ever comes, it will require clear eyes and reliable maps, not faith in a carving the original author would no longer recognise.
