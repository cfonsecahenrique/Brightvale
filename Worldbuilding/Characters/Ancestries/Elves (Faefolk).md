# [[Elves (Faefolk)]]

**Elvenborne** are generally seen as detached from the worldly, day-to-day political affairs of mortal races, but they are not a monolithic culture of mourning. They follow diverse philosophies regarding their severed connection to the **[[Fae]]**. Some believe rigorous study of Aetherweaving at [[Aurore]]'s Peak might one day reopen the Faewilds. Others are deeply scholarly, believing the "how and why" of the [[Apostasy]] can be found in the ruins of the Armisian Empire or even older, forgotten worlds. Still others—often referred to as **Urban [[Elves (Faefolk)|Elves]]** living in [[Medsen]] or marrying into High Houses—have detached themselves entirely from their past, preferring to carve a brighter, forward-looking future rather than dwelling on tragedy.

**Naming Traditions (Celtic):** *Cillian, Ciara, Eira, Maeve, Aodhán, Faolan, Fíadh, Elowen, Enriel, Cirdan, Hwynfer, *Taliesin*.*

## Elven Magic — The Formulaic Tradition

Elven magic is built on **reason**. Where human mages historically reached for raw instinct or faith, Elven practitioners study the underlying logic of a phenomenon until the spell becomes an inevitable conclusion rather than an act of will. To make water boil, an Elven mage understands heat transfer. To move air, they understand pressure and void. The magic follows from the understanding.

This yields precision and surprising reach — a mage who grasps the principle behind one effect can extrapolate to related effects that would take other traditions decades of practice. The cost is equally distinctive: the logical architecture that enables such precision can become a prison. A mage who has built a perfectly consistent internal framework may find themselves unable to advance past a certain point — not for lack of power, but because their own reasoning has ruled something out. Elven scholars call these **Arcane Locks**, and they are considered the tradition's defining hazard. Breaking one typically requires recognizing it as a self-imposed constraint rather than a genuine limit, then finding the crack in the logic — or abandoning logic entirely, at least momentarily.

Elven scholars have long described the old human approach to magic — pure instinct and belief, no framework at all — with the somewhat ambivalent phrase *the wisdom of humans*. The respect in it is genuine. What humans could accomplish in their most desperate, unthinking moments has rarely been replicated by any formulaic practitioner.
