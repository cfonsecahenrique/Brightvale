# Of Ancient Days, and the Light That Endures

*This account is drawn from fragments: charred manuscripts salvaged from ruined libraries, songs remembered by elven survivors who would not speak of what they witnessed, and the mural-carvings found deep within the Beacon Temple, whose meaning the scholars of Medsen still argue over. What is known is set down here. What is suspected lingers between these lines. What has been forgotten may yet determine whether we survive.*

---

## I. The World Before

In an age beyond the reach of living memory, when the sun rose gold and unhurried over a world that did not yet know what it was capable of losing, there were peoples and kingdoms more numerous than the stars reflected in the old sea.

Greatest of all human endeavors was the **Armisian Empire**, which rose over centuries in the broad hearthlands of the known world. It was a civilization of terrible ambition and genuine brilliance — a place where reason and arcane knowledge were not merely tools but the sacred pillars of governance itself. Its Emperor did not rule through birthright alone, but through the Bloodright: an ancient compact of dynastic magic older than the Empire's name, binding the **Noble Houses** to the throne through invisible chains of power and obligation. In the schools of **Armis-Caen**, its luminous capital, scholars transcribed spells lost to other peoples, and mages rose to seats of counsel beside generals and lords. The Empire was imperfect — proud, grasping, hierarchical in ways that crushed as often as they elevated — but it was *alive* with an energy that those who survived it would mourn for the rest of their days.

Beyond the Empire's heartland, and far across the **Vast Salt Sea** that no map made today can fully name, came the **Dwarves** — stocky, suspicious, and magnificently practical. They crossed in great stone-hulled vessels, driven by reasons their descendants speak of only obliquely: some calamity, perhaps, in their distant homeland, or perhaps simply a compulsion toward the new. They settled along the coasts and mountain-roots of the Armisian world, building compact communities of trade and craft, neither subjects of the Empire nor fully separate from it. Their iron and stonework were prized. Their councils were their own.

The **Elves** moved through that golden age in two distinct currents. Some dwelt in the great cities and imperial courts as advisors and loremasters, their long memory a living library for human ambition. Others had never left the wild places at all — building settlements not of stone but of *living wood and glamoured hillside*, communities that merged at their edges with the **Faewilds**, the vast parallel realm of old magic that bordered reality like a dream borders waking. These forest elves did not merely live beside the Fae; they spoke with them, learned from them, drew strength from a connection as old as the land itself.

And there were the **Orcs**, who came not in ships but on the long roads from distant steppes, in great nomadic bands of horse and cart, their covered wagons forming temporary cities that rose and dissolved like smoke. They traded where they were welcomed, fought where they were hired, and moved on. The Empire, ever pragmatic, employed many as mercenary soldiers — and those Orc war-bands earned their fierce reputation a hundred times over. They belonged to no kingdom, and no kingdom fully belonged to them.

There were **Halflings** in the old world too, in their quiet Shires of field and furrow, their festivals and their harvests, living in a peace that larger peoples often mistook for smallness. And there were others — Ancestries now scattered, diminished, some entirely gone. The world was wide and varied in those days.

The world was not perfect. But it *was*.

## II. The Apostasy

Then came the event that undid it.

Its causes are not known. Its exact nature is debated by every scholar who has survived long enough to debate anything. The word used for it — *the Apostasy* — implies an abandonment, a betrayal of covenant. Whether this means a betrayal by the gods, *of* the gods, or by something else entirely, no record agrees upon. There are those who believe the Emperor of Armis-Caen trespassed into forbidden magic in his hunger for immortality. There are those who speak of a war between divine beings that shattered something fundamental about the world's fabric. There are those who say nothing at all, and look away.

What can be said is this: the sky changed. A crimson veil suffused the heavens — not a sunset, not a storm, but a permanent dimming of the light, as though some vast hand had drawn gauze across the sun. Seasons began to fail. Winters arrived early and stayed too long. Summers became a pallid memory, warm enough only to remember what warmth had once been.

That alone might have been survived. Civilizations have weathered cold before.

But the sky-change was only the first wound.

In the wake of the Apostasy, magic itself became erratic, then dangerous, then catastrophic in the hands of those who did not understand what had changed. The spell-wars that followed — kingdom against kingdom, house against house, mage against mage — reshaped entire landscapes. Mountains were unmade. Rivers were boiled away or redirected into deserts. The great spell-scars of those wars can still be glimpsed from high altitude, if one dares look down from the Teardrop Peaks into the Wastes below: geometries of ruin too vast to have been merely natural, too deliberate to have been mere disaster.

And through every crack opened by war, the **Umbra** crept in.

It came as shadow — not the shadow of clouds or dusk, but a *living* darkness, an indigo-black miasma that settled into the lowest places first, into valleys and river-beds and the basements of ruined cities, gathering like still water after a flood. It was not mindless. It was not exactly alive, in any sense the living would recognize. But it *reached* — for warmth, for light, for the vital spark of living things — and what it touched, it remade.

Trees became brittle and black. Beasts lost their shapes. The dead did not stay dead. Those who fell in the growing dark rose again as **Revenants** — empty, cold, and purposeful in a way that made scholars shudder, for a Revenant retained enough cognition to *hunt*. And the longer a creature spent near the Umbra's heart, the more it changed: swelling into monstrous forms, losing the memory of what it had once been, becoming an extension of the shadow's patient hunger.

The Elves felt the Faewilds wither and close. One by one, the doorways between the mortal world and the realm of old magic sealed themselves, or were simply consumed by the encroaching dark. The elven forest-settlements, which had drawn sustenance from that connection for millennia, found themselves suddenly and terribly alone — cut off from the magic that had been as natural to them as breath. Many did not survive the grief of it.

The Dwarves withdrew underground, then found that underground was no sanctuary — that the Umbra crept through rock as readily as air, contaminating deep places, mutating cave-life into horrors. They sealed their doors and fought in the dark.

The Orc bands — rootless, mobile — found their perpetual wandering had become desperate flight, with nowhere left to flee *to*. They had no great cities to fall back to, no archives of lore to guide them. They had each other, and the road, and the road was running out.

And the Empire of Armis-Caen — that brilliant, terrible human marvel — fractured along every fault line its ambition had created. The Noble Houses, bound to a throne that was increasingly powerless to protect them, turned on one another in the chaos. Rebellions. Successions. Blood compacts weaponized. Cities that had stood for centuries fell in seasons. The last Emperors are not recorded, only whispered of, and those whispers disagree entirely on how the end came.

*This is the shape of the unmaking. We do not know its reason. We know only its weight.*

## III. The Long Withering

For years — scholars arguing for decades in the Medsen archives have never agreed on precisely how many — the peoples of the world endured.

Endured is the right word. Not thrived. Not adapted. *Endured*.

Pockets of humanity clung to defensible high ground or fortified ruins, burning their furniture for warmth and their books for light, fighting each other as often as they fought the dark. Elven enclaves drew their glamours tight around them and refused all contact, preserving what magic they could within their dwindling circles of old stone. Dwarven communities, if they survived at all, did so in sealed warrens lit by lantern and determination, their trade-routes dissolved, their great sea-vessels rotting on shores now choked with Umbra-mist.

The nomadic Orc bands did perhaps better than any, at first — their mobility kept them ahead of the advancing dark, their martial culture gave them the skills to fight what others could not. But there is only so far a road can run, and eventually every road in the known world had an Umbra-shadow across it.

And then there were those who did not merely survive, but made bargains to survive — men and women of once-noble houses who, in their desperation, struck covenants with the powers that lingered at the edge of the collapsing world: **Devils** of cold contract, **Demons** of raw and hungry will. The terms of these pacts are not entirely known, but their mark has been passed down through blood: sons and daughters born with red skin and dark horns, or with the pale gray-blue complexion and sweeping horns of the **Infernis** — the **Tieflings**. Cursed, some said. Blessed, said others. *Touched*, all agreed.

This is how civilizations die: not in one great catastrophe, but in the accumulation of smaller surrenders, each one reasonable in its moment, until the moment when nothing remains but the surrenders themselves.

## IV. The Dawnfather

We do not know his name.

This is perhaps the strangest wound of all — that the figure who preserved what remains of the world is known to us only by the title his followers gave him after the fact: the **Dawnfather**. Whether he was human or of some other ancestry, whether he was born to power or grew toward it in the chaos — these things have been debated, mythologized, and contradicted so many times that the truth, if it ever existed in written form, has not survived.

What is known, or believed, or perhaps half-remembered in the wordless way that certain things are known without being told:

*He gathered them.*

In an age when every surviving leader was hoarding what little remained to their own people — sealing gates, turning away refugees, protecting *theirs* at the expense of *everyone else's* — this figure moved through the ruins and collected the remnants of what had been. Humans and Elves. Dwarves who had emerged blinking from their sealed warrens. Halflings dispossessed of their Shires. Orc warriors who had run out of road. Tieflings whose bargained heritage made them unwelcome in settlements that should have been glad of any help.

He did not command them. Or if he did, those who followed him do not speak of it that way. They speak of *following*, willingly, because he *moved toward something* when everything else was running away.

What he moved toward was the **Teardrop Mountain Range** — a great arc of impassable peaks at the northern edge of the known world, shaped on ancient maps like a teardrop fallen from the eye of some vast, uncaring sky. Most who had looked at those peaks had seen only impossibility: the passes cruel, the gorges plummeting, the altitude itself lethal to the unprepared, and the Umbra-corrupted creatures that now haunted the mountain approaches worse than any of these.

The journey was, by all accounts that survived it, a miracle and a horror in near-equal measure. The passes killed some. The cold took others. In the deep gorges where the wind dropped to an unnatural stillness, the Umbra pressed close, and Revenants descended in the dark. There were days when the column of survivors nearly broke — nearly abandoned the march, turned back, accepted whatever end awaited below. The Dawnfather, by whatever means, kept them moving.

They came through — fewer than they had been, but alive — into a valley that seemed, at first, impossible.

## V. Brightvale

It was a plateau hidden in the mountain's embrace, broad and sheltered and eerily *quiet* — the kind of quiet that feels, after long enough in the noise of ruin, like holding your breath. The **Teardrop Range** surrounded it on all sides, sheer walls of stone that the Umbra had never found a way over. To the north stood the **Frost-Veil Peaks**, guarding the way to the icy white deserts beyond; to the south lay the **Idol Hollows**, through which the Umbra-tainted perpetually sought a way up from the lowlands. The altitude alone — the thin, cold air of the high peaks — seemed to hold the miasma below the rim. The valley breathed.

They called it **Brightvale**. Or perhaps it named itself, in time. The records disagree, as records do.

At its northern end, the Dawnfather performed his final act.

What ritual he practiced is not recorded in any text that has survived. The mural-carvings in the Temple walls depict something — a figure kneeling, arms raised, light pouring upward from a wound in the stone — but the scholars argue endlessly over whether this is literal or allegorical, historical or devotional, accurate or invented. What is agreed upon is the result:

*He ceased to exist as a person, and the* **Beacon** *began.*

A finger of pure, eternal light, rising from the northern heart of the valley, visible from every point within the plateau, warm in a way that the sun had ceased to be. It did not flicker. It did not diminish. And where its light fell, the Umbra could not follow — the curse of unlife it carried dissolved at the Beacon's edge like morning frost.

The survivors built a temple around the place where he had knelt — first a simple ring of gathered stones, then over the generations a vast and soaring structure of arches and towers, built to house not only the Beacon but the Caretakers who tended it and the great **Aetherweavers** who learned, over long decades, to focus it: to strengthen its reach, to direct its warmth, to use it as both shield and light-house against the dark.

The valley *bloomed*.

The **Sacred Valley**, the lower portions of the plateau where the Beacon's influence was warmest and the soil deepest, became something the survivors had not dared imagine: fertile ground. **Halflings**, whose traditions were rooted in the soil as deeply as any crop, found here what they had lost with the Shires — and set about re-creating it with the methodical patience of a people who understand that some things cannot be rushed. Fields appeared. Orchards. Small stone houses with wide hearths and well-worn doorsteps. The halfling farming communities became the quiet, vital foundation that fed a civilization learning to exist again.

In the deep forests of the plateau's interior, where ancient stone circles of uncertain age stood half-swallowed by moss and root, the **Elves** found something they had not expected: *a trace*. The Fae connection was gone — the Apostasy had seen to that, had closed the doors between worlds so thoroughly that most elves had ceased to believe they could ever be reopened. But in the stones of Brightvale, latent with a magic that predated even the oldest elven records, there was a *resonance* — a frequency that whispered of old pathways not entirely sealed. They settled near those circles and began the long, painstaking work of listening.

The **Dwarves**, ever practical, looked east to where the Teardrop peaks rose within the valley's boundary and found in them what they needed: rich veins of iron and salt-stone and the peculiar blue mineral they called *coldfire*, useful in a dozen arcane applications. They named their eastern mountain town **Hvergi** — a word from their sea-crossing tongue meaning, roughly, *neither here nor there*, a name that carried a Dwarven humor too dry for humans to always recognize.

The **Humans** and **Tieflings** — mixed, various, bringing the remembered architectural ambitions of lost Armis-Caen — built the city that grew around the Dawnfather's Temple. They named it **Medsen**, though the old name *Emberdawn* lingered in common speech for generations. It rose in angles and arches that echoed the great Imperial Cities: not quite the same, never quite the same, but reaching toward that remembered light with the desperate hope of people building something that must *last*. Noble Houses reformed, drawing on old bloodlines and older obligations, arguing over precedence in the shadow of everything they had survived. The city grew. Walls went up. Markets opened.

Further along the valley, at the foot of the great **Aurore's Peak** — where the night skies blazed green with the winter auroras, those curtains of light the Aetherweavers would later conclude were visible manifestations of the Weave itself — the town of **Aurore** was built. Scholars and magic-workers gathered there, constructing first a tower, then a full **Academy** where the art of Aetherweaving could be studied and advanced; where the lost knowledge of the Armisian mage-schools could be slowly reconstructed from the memory of survivors and the mural-carvings and the charred margins of half-eaten manuscripts.

The **Orcs**, restless even in safety, settled *among* others rather than apart from them — in every settlement of Brightvale, there were Orc families, Orc traders, Orc veterans who had held the rear on the mountain march and never forgotten what that had cost them. Many became **Venturers** — the term that emerged, over time, for those who chose to descend back into the wasteland below, through the passes, into the ruin of the world, in search of whatever might be found.

The entrance to the valley — the point at which the **Venturer's Trail** (as the mountain passage came to be named, in honor of those who came first and those who came after) reached the plateau — was fortified into a town-fortress: **Emberfort**. Here the **Emberguard** kept watch, monitoring the passes for threats descending from the peaks and for survivors still, sometimes, making their way up. Here too the Venturers made their base — a community of the restless and the driven and the curious, who organized their descents from this place and returned to it with whatever they had found or failed to find in the world below.

That path, the one the Dawnfather had led them up — treacherous and cold and Umbra-haunted — had become the only road between the Last Civilization and everything it had lost.

## VI. The Guilds, and the Shape of Now

The centuries since have not been without their own struggles.

The **Noble Houses**, reconstructed from the wreckage of Armisian precedent, retained their titles and their old pride, but found that in the enclosed world of Brightvale, blood alone could not feed a population. Real power drifted — as power tends to do — toward those who controlled the things that survival required: the rations, the light, the knowledge.

The **Guilds** grew to fill this space. The **Caretakers of the Beacon** held the holy fire that made life in the valley possible. The **Aetherweaver Academy** held the knowledge that made the fire useful and the walls defensible. The **Slayers** dealt with what crept in through the cracks. The **Emberguard** kept order. And the more indispensable the guilds became, the more the old hierarchies of House and title chafed against them.

The tension between Guild and House defines the politics of Medsen today: a long, careful contest of precedence and resource, fought in Council chambers and market negotiations and the occasional quiet removal of an inconvenient person before they could become an inconvenient problem. It is not war. It has not yet been war. But it has the shape of something that could become one, given the right pressure.

---

## VII. The Flickering

The pressure, as it happens, may be arriving.

There are those — not merely the superstitious, but the Aetherweavers themselves, who measure these things — who have noticed that the Beacon is not entirely what it was. Its light, they say carefully, *wavers*. Imperceptibly, perhaps. Not visibly to the naked eye in the moments between glances. But in the instruments, in the careful measurements of output and reach, something has changed. The Beacon is — not failing. No one yet dares say *failing*. But it is *not the same* as it was, and no one can explain why the soul of the Dawnfather, which has sustained this light for centuries unchanged, should begin to alter now.

And then the earthquake came.

It shook the known world — not violently enough to crack Brightvale's walls or tumble Aurore's Peak, but enough to be felt in the bones of every living thing on the plateau, enough to change the landscape of the Wastes below in ways that the Venturers who descended afterward found both confusing and alarming. Mountains had shifted. The paths marked on the best maps were no longer where the maps said they should be.

And in the Umbra-saturated lowlands, far below and to the east of the known Venturer's routes, the earthquake had opened something. A *scar* in the earth — a gash where the ground had subsided, pulling the land apart to reveal what lay beneath it. From the expedition that went to look, and from the members of it who came back, two things were agreed upon:

The scar descended into a cave complex of enormous depth. And from far below, from distances the expedition's lights could not reach, something vast had been disturbed.

What it is, no one yet knows.

What it *means*, the scholars of Medsen are afraid to speculate on.

---

## VIII. What Remains to Be Done

*This, then, is the shape of the world as we have received it:*

A valley of light, sustained by a dying god's sacrifice, in which the last remnants of a world that destroyed itself have built something fragile and worth saving. Above, the Beacon shivers. Below, the dark stirs in a new way. Between the two, the long trail of stone and memory called the Venturer's Trail connects the safety of Brightvale to everything that was lost — and perhaps, if the old scholars are right, to everything that might yet be found.

*The answers are not within these walls.*

They are in the ruined libraries of Armis-Caen, buried under decades of Umbra-growth. In the sealed vaults of Dwarven warrens that may no longer have anyone living inside them. In the cave-mouths of the scar, where something old and massive waits in the dark. In the elven stones that the Fae connection once flowed through, and might yet flow through again. In the unexplored valleys and unmapped ruins of a world that was, before the Apostasy took it, as wide and various as the sky.

They will not come to Brightvale on their own.

*Go down, then, Venturers. Go down into the ruin and the dark. Not because it is safe — it is not. Not because it is certain — nothing below is certain. But because the light that holds this valley together needs tending, and the only tending it can receive now is knowledge. And knowledge is out there, waiting in the places where no one has yet been brave enough to look.*

*Go down. Go wide. Come back, when you can.*

*Something depends on it.*
