# Of Ancient Days — Narrator's Monologue

*~4 minutes. Approx. 560 words.*

---

There was a world, once.

Wide and warm, lit by a sun that did not apologize for its brightness. Humans built an Empire on knowledge and blood and ambition — a civilization of reason and arcane power, its cities gleaming with marble and spell-light, its Noble Houses bound to the Throne by chains of magic old enough to have forgotten why they were forged. Dwarves crossed a sea no map fully names, driven by some calamity in their distant homeland that their descendants still do not discuss, settling in the mountain-roots to trade stone and craft for the coins of men. Elves moved in two currents — some in the courts and cities as advisors and loremasters, others in the wild places, where they walked between the mortal world and the Faewilds as easily as crossing a threshold, one foot always in the older place. And Orcs — whose war-bands the Empire employed and whose culture the Empire underestimated until the two were too entangled to separate — moved the long roads between everything, belonging to no kingdom and shaping all of them.

It was imperfect. Every part of it was imperfect. But it *was*.

Then came the Apostasy.

No one alive can say what truly happened. The word itself means *betrayal* — whether of the gods, by the gods, or by something that wore their name. What can be said is this: the sky changed. A crimson veil across the heavens — not a storm, not a sunset, but a permanent dimming, as though the sun had been wounded and refused to heal. Seasons began to fail. And through every crack opened by the spell-wars that followed — kingdom against kingdom, house against house, magic turned catastrophic in the hands of those who could not understand what had changed — the Umbra crept in.

A living darkness. An indigo-black miasma that settled into the lowest places first, into valleys and riverbeds and the basements of ruined cities, patient and reaching. What it touched, it remade. Trees became black glass. Beasts lost their shape. The dead rose, cold and purposeful, as Revenants. And the Faewilds — the vast parallel realm the Elves had walked in for millennia — sealed itself, instantly, completely, as if a door had been slammed from the other side. The Elves found themselves utterly alone, cut off from the magic that had been as natural to them as breath.

Civilizations withered. The great fell first. The small followed. Some of the desperate made bargains with powers at the edges of the collapsing world — Devils and Demons of cold contract and raw will — and the mark of those bargains passed into their blood and their children's blood. Pale gray skin and dark horns. Red flesh and burning eyes. The Tieflings: touched, and surviving, when others were not.

There was no safe place. Only survivors.

And then there was one figure — whose name we have lost, though his deed we have not — who did the thing no leader of a dying world is supposed to do. He gathered them: Human and Elf, Dwarf and Halfling, Orc and Tiefling, the desperate and the lost and the ones who had simply run out of road. And he led them through the killing cold of the Idol Hollows, through passes no one had crossed in living memory, through gorge and freezing dark and the reaching of the Umbra at their heels — into a valley the shadow could not follow.

A plateau, broad and sheltered, cradled in impassable peaks.

There, at the valley's northern heart, he gave himself entirely. Blood, body, soul — offered to a ritual no one else has ever been able to name or repeat. In his place rose the Beacon: a pillar of eternal light, warm where the sun had grown cold, alive where the world had learned to die.

We named the valley Brightvale. We built a city around the place where he knelt and called it Medsen. We built an Academy on the peaks above Aurore, where the night sky blazes green, and there we learned to tend the Beacon's fire — to extend its reach, to use it as shield and lighthouse. Halflings planted fields in the warm southern valley. Elves settled near the old stones in the forests, where something in the ancient circles whispered of pathways not entirely sealed, and began the long work of listening for what the Faewilds had left behind. Dwarves carved Hvergi from the eastern peaks. And at the mountain entrance, where the Venturer's Trail meets the plateau, Emberfort was raised — gateway and garrison, and the home of those restless souls who descend, again and again, into the ruined world below.

We called ourselves the Enduring. For generations, we were right to.

The centuries since have had their own struggles. The Noble Houses reformed, drawing on old bloodlines and older debts, arguing over precedence in a world too small for all their pride. And as the Guilds grew — the Caretakers who tend the holy fire, the Aetherweavers who study it, the Slayers who hunt what crept through the cracks, the Emberguard who keep the peace, the Explorers who map the Wastes and carry back whatever the old world forgot to bury — real power drifted toward those who controlled the things survival requires. The Monarchs, the Sun-Born heirs of the Dawnfather's line, became slowly, inevitably, ceremonial. The current King has not been seen in seven years, a ghost behind the walls of his own temple. The Council governs now, in chambers where Guild and House wage a careful, bloodless war that has not yet become an actual one.

But the Beacon flickers. The instruments in the Academy measure it: something has changed in that undying fire, and no one can explain what. And the earth cracked open in the year just past — a great scar in the Umbra-soaked lowlands, leading down into depths no map has ever reached, where something vast and old has been disturbed by the shaking of the ground.

The answers are not within these walls. They never were.

They wait in ruined libraries and sealed vaults, in shadow-choked passes and the unknown dark below the Chasm. Someone has to go find them.

That is what Venturers are for.

Go down. Go wide. *Come back.*

Something depends on it.
