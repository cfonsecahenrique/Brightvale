# The Conquest of An'Hiseret — The War Against the Dead

How does a living empire conquer one with **infinite soldiers** — a death-cult that raises every corpse into a tireless, fearless, sleepless warrior, and never runs out of corpses? The answer is the defining military story of the golden age, and it is not a story of strength. It is a story of a moat, a weapon, and a single moment of enemy weakness seized without mercy.

The short of it: **Armis did not defeat the dead. It survived the dead for centuries, gained a weapon, and then struck in the one generation An'Hiseret was busy killing itself.**

---

## The Long Stalemate (before 744 AF)

For centuries Armis and [[An'Hiseret]] were natural enemies, separated by a stretch of the southern Central Sea and by an abhorrence that made peace unthinkable. The enmity is older even than that: it was **Amun-Atir's seizure of the Seretis** — turning humanity's southern cradle to the death-cult — that drove the other gods to withdraw northward and nurture the peoples who founded Armis, Caen, and Himilkos. The two empires were enemies from the moment the second one existed.

**The religious wall.** To the Armisians — who lay their dead to rest and honour their ancestors at the household hearth — the An'Hisereti enslavement of the dead is the ultimate blasphemy: the beloved departed denied their rest and conscripted into eternal labour. To the An'Hisereti, the Armisians are obscene wastrels who **burn and drown** perfectly good dead — and in An'Hiseret, oblivion is the *death penalty*. Each people believed the other guilty of the worst sin imaginable. Coexistence was never on the table.

**The moat, and the thing about the dead.** What kept Armis alive was the sea — and a simple, decisive fact: **the dead do not swim.** Swimming is a survival instinct, and the Mindless have no instincts; they cannot learn it and cannot be driven to it. An'Hiseret could raise a host to blacken the desert and never put a single husk across open water. So the death-empire's infinite army stopped at its own shore.

But the wall worked both ways. Whenever Armis tried to *land* and carry the war to the Seretian coast, its soldiers met the endless dead the moment they hit the beach and were overwhelmed. Armis was safe and Armis was stuck: strong enough to survive forever, never strong enough to win.

---

## The Awakening Changes Everything (744 AF)

The [[Aetherweave Mastery|Aetherweave Awakening]] turned the passive moat into an active weapon. Once the Empire could **command Water**, the dead's one weakness became a blade in Armisian hands:

- **Drowning the legions.** Aetherwrights collapsed tidal causeways crowded with marching dead, raised waves to scour husk-ranks off the coast, and denied the river-fords the dead relied on. Water that the dead could not cross could now be *brought to them*.
- **Starving the necromancers.** An'Hiseret is a river-and-flood civilisation; its life depends on the sacred annual flood. Command of Water let Armis **sabotage the flood itself** — and famine starves the *living*, the priests and god-kings who alone can command the dead. You do not fight an infinite army; you starve the handful of throats that give it orders.
- **A new way of war.** From the enemy's water-weakness grew a whole Armisian amphibious doctrine — marine landings, river-fleets, delta-strikes — that would serve the Empire for centuries after.

The Awakening gave Armis the *means*. It still needed an *opening*.

---

## The Decapitation Insight

The opening was possible because of a truth the Empire's generals came to understand: **the infinite army is not autonomous. It is a network.** The Mindless have no will of their own; each is steered by a living — or souled — controller through the necromantic Weave. A high-priest commands hundreds; a God-King, tens of thousands. **Sever the controllers and their dead go inert or feral.** Armis did not need to destroy a million corpses. It needed to kill the few who held the leashes.

And then An'Hiseret killed most of them for it.

---

## The Civil War of the Amun (the opening)

An'Hiseret's god-kings were the souled dead — raised with mind and soul intact to rule across centuries. Chief among them reigned **Khefru-amun**, God-King for **six hundred years**, and his brother **[[The Return of Xol-amun|Xol-amun]]** — both sons of Amun-Atir, both demi-gods, both deathless.

There lay the flaw at the heart of the death-empire: **a God-King never dies, so there is no succession, ever.** Xol-amun, the younger, could never inherit while his brother endured — and his brother would endure forever. Six centuries of that resentment finally broke into open war. To kill a deathless rival each brother had to learn how to *truly unmake a bound soul* — forbidden knowledge that, once loosed, does not go back in its jar. They turned it on each other, and in the end they **destroyed one another**: Khefru-amun unmade, and Xol-amun the technical victor.

The war did to the An'Hisereti command-network what no Armisian army ever could. Two demi-gods and a great many souled high-priests annihilated each other, and across the empire the dead they had commanded simply **switched off** — vast host-fragments going leaderless, inert, or feral in a single season of chaos.

---

## The Conquest (~900 AF)

Armis struck into that chaos with everything the Awakening had given it. A naval invasion drove up the great river and the delta; Aetherwrights washed the disordered dead from the Empire's path and severed the surviving controllers; marine columns seized the holy sites one by one. Critically, they reached the high sanctum **mid-rite** — interrupting Xol-amun's own soul-binding, the ritual meant to seat the victor in a restored, eternal body. The rite was never completed.

An'Hiseret, the unconquerable death-empire, fell in a handful of years — not to Armisian might, but to Armisian *timing*. The official histories call it the triumph of the living over the deathless. They are quieter about how much of the work the An'Hisereti had already done themselves.

> **The rumour.** It is officially nonsense, and it will not quite die: that Armisian agents did not merely *exploit* the brothers' war but quietly *fanned* it — a whisper, a forged insult, a well-placed lie six hundred years in the making. The Empire denies it. The denial is very firm.

---

## The Uneasy Peace

Winning the south was easier than holding it, because **you cannot un-make a civilisation built on the dead.**

- **The gray-area ban.** Armis outlawed the death-cult and the raising of the dead — but the entire economy and infrastructure of the south *is* undead labour. Abolishing it overnight would collapse the Empire's own new province. So the ban became a line that is more theory than practice: **"no *new* dead"** — forbid fresh raising and (above all) the souled-undeath rite, while turning a blind eye to the existing Mindless workforce that predates the conquest. Enforcement is uneven, corrupt, and quietly resented from both sides.
- **The hypocrisy.** Worse: some Armisian governors and houses in the south have come to *depend* on the tireless dead, commissioning undead labour through cut-outs. The very thing the Empire conquered the south to abolish, it now profits from in the dark.
- **The Necropolis it cannot catch.** The greatest standing challenge to imperial rule is the **[[The Wandering Necropolis|Moving Necropolis]]** — the corpse-hauled wandering city, which rolls on across the deep southern desert. Armisian power is naval power, and the Necropolis keeps to the dry interior where no fleet can sail and no river runs. The sea that won Armis the coast cannot touch the walking tomb. It grinds on, beyond reach, carrying the death-cult's deepest purpose with it.

The south is conquered, garrisoned, and taxed. It is not, in any sense that matters, *pacified*.

## Hooks (GM)

- The "no new dead" line is a permanent moral mess a venturer can be sent to enforce, exploit, or expose — especially where Armisian profit hides behind it.
- The forbidden knowledge of *unmaking a bound soul*, born of the brothers' war, is loose somewhere. Whoever holds it can end a god.
- The Moving Necropolis is the unreachable enemy: a dungeon, a faction, and a countdown that the Empire's whole way of war cannot address.
