# Armis-Caen

The **twin capital** of **[[The Armisian Empire]]** — once two rival cities, now one sprawling, half-merged metropolis ringed by impossibly tall white stone walls. **[[Armis]]** was the conqueror; **[[Caen]]** was the prize; today the old boundary between them is a seam of tension and ambition rather than a wall. The combined city is the Empire's political, religious, and martial heart: the place all roads, all sea lanes, and all returning expeditions ultimately answer to.

> **Inspiration:** **Bronze Age Hellenic and Phoenician** city at its apex — the grandeur of Carthage before the fall, the civic density of Athens, the palatial excess of Minoan Knossos. White stone. Salt air. The smell of money and war.

## The City Seen from the Air

![[Old Armis.webp]]

> *Armis-Caen on the sea approach, mid-morning: what a gull sees, and what the crow's nest sees an hour before the harbour does.*

The first thing that undoes a visitor's expectations is that Armis-Caen is not *a* city on *a* shore. It is a **lagoon capital** — a scatter of islands, spits, and reclaimed banks strung across a wide sheltered bay, each one built edge to edge until the stone runs out and the water begins. From above it reads as pale limestone and terracotta laid over turquoise, and the channels between the wards are as busy as any avenue in it.

### The Water
Shallows over sand glow a luminous blue-green; the deep-water channels run hard sapphire, and you can read the sailing lanes off the colour alone. The water is *everywhere* — no ward of the capital is more than a few streets from a quay, and the bay is threaded with wakes: skiffs, lighters, ferry-boats, oared tenders crossing between islands on errands too small to record.

### The Seawalls
Every island of consequence is belted at the waterline by a **white curtain wall** rising straight out of the sea, unbroken for hundreds of paces at a stretch and broad enough to walk. Square **bastion towers** stand at intervals along it, each crowned with a shallow **verdigris dome** — weathered copper gone soft green against all that white — so the walls read from a distance as a dotted line of pale blocks and green caps. The walls do not enclose the city; they enclose its *wards*, one by one. What the Armisians call their concentric ideal survives here as an archipelago's version of it: ring after ring of walled ground, with water where a lesser empire would have put a moat.

### The Near Ward and the Acropolis
The great island in the foreground is the densest thing in the known world: **terracotta roofs packed shoulder to shoulder**, stepping up and inland in tight terraces, the streets visible only as thin shadowed seams between them. At its crown sits the civic and sacred complex — a **colonnaded temple with a pedimented portico and a red-tiled roof**, flanked by lower halls, a **green-domed building**, and one of the few pieces of open green in the whole ward: a walled garden court, the rarest luxury in Armis-Caen.

### The Temple Islands
Out in the middle water, on its own island, stands a second great **white-columned temple under red tile**, so sited that it is seen whole from the sea with nothing behind it — a deliberate piece of imperial staging. Housing crowds right up to its precinct wall and then stops dead at the sacred boundary. Others like it dot the bay; the capital's gods keep their own islands, and attending one's festival means hiring a boat.

### The Lighthouses and the Moles
Where the channels narrow, **pale freestanding towers** rise directly from the water on stone moles — some are lighthouses, some are chain-towers, and from the deck of an approaching ship there is no telling which until you are close enough for it to matter. **Long causeways and embankments** run dead straight between islands, thin white lines across the blue, carrying the roads that the water would otherwise have cut.

### The Far Citadel
Miles off, at the neck of the bay, a rocky headland carries a **walled palace-citadel** — towers, terraces, and a keep-mass of pale stone above the strait, commanding the approach. Everything that enters the capital passes beneath it first.

### The Horizon
The bay is closed in by **forested green headlands**, then rising ridges, then a far wall of **blue-grey mountains with snow still on them**. The Empire's heart sits in a bowl: sea in front, mountains all around, weather coming in over both.

### Ships and Light
On the deep-water approach, **big lateen-rigged merchantmen** come up under tan sail, three and four in the lane at once, while a lone small boat cuts across mid-channel on some errand of its own. The light is high, hazy and washed out — sun through sea-haze, the far distance bleaching almost to white, the whole city looking less built than *watercoloured* onto the bay.

**What it says about the Armisians:** they did not choose a defensible hill and grow outward from it. They took a bay, walled every scrap of land in it, bridged what they could, and left the rest to boats — then spent the surplus on temples sited to be admired from the water by the people arriving to pay them money.

## The Walls

The defining first impression of Armis-Caen is its walls: **white limestone, impossibly tall**, polished to reflect sun and sea so that the city can be seen from the horizon an hour before landfall. The Armisian ideal is **circular** — concentric rings of walled wards diminishing toward the centre — but the bay refuses it. What the city actually has is that ideal broken across an archipelago: **each island belted in its own white circuit**, the seaward faces running straight and unbroken along the quay lines, the water standing in for every ring the builders could not close. The outermost circuits are thick enough for a road along the crest. **Square bastion towers under shallow verdigris domes** crown them at regular intervals, and the towers carry signal fires; the smoke of the nearest relay carries a message across the water to the palace in minutes — which in a city cut into pieces by channels is not ceremony but necessity.

## The Harbour Quarter

### The Cothon
The **military harbour** of Armis-Caen — circular, enclosed, and controlled. Smaller than the great Cothon of **[[Pattis]]**, but it is the original, the template every other Armisian port aspired to. Access is through a single narrow channel, closed by a chain at night.

### The Neoria
Ringing the Cothon's inner wall: **covered ship sheds**, their vaulted arch-mouths gaping at the water. Each berths a war galley under roof, dry-docked on timber rollers, its oars racked and counted. Visitors arriving by sea see the neoria before they see the temples.

### The Emporion
The **commercial harbour**, separate from the Cothon and deliberately so — a district of warehouses, bonded counting houses, foreign merchant enclaves (each with a small shrine to their own gods), and the *xenodochion* where dignitary guests are housed at imperial expense. It smells of salt, cedar, dye, and spice. The Emporion never fully sleeps.

### The Lighthouse
At the harbour mouth: a **tall, white, octagonal tower** — an Armisian octagon being as close to a circle as a tower can practically be — with a polished bronze mirror at its crown that throws a beam visible forty stades out to sea. The lighthouse keeper is an imperial appointment for life. There is a persistent rumour that the light has never once gone dark, even during the sack of Caen.

## The Civic Heart

### The Great Agora
The city's **central public circle** — a vast open plaza at the hub of the radial avenues. Ringed by *stoas*, the long colonnaded porticoes where merchants, advocates, and philosophers conduct business under the same roof. The Agora is where proclamations are read from the *bema* (a raised stone speaker's platform), where public trials convene, and where the grain price is set each morning. On festival days it fills with altars, incense, and crowds. On ordinary days it is merely the loudest place in the known world.

### The Bouleuterion
Beside the Agora: a **small, severe semicircular chamber**, tiered in stone, where the city's council of suffetes and senior senators deliberate. No paintings, no dedications. The austerity is the point — law strips away ornament. The public may observe from the upper tiers. Decisions made here hold the weight of the Empire.

### The Theatre
On a natural slope of the inner ring, facing the agora: a **carved stone theatre**, its tiered cavea accommodating thousands. Tragedies and comedies are performed at religious festivals; orators and sophists hire it on off-days. The acoustics are considered the finest in the Empire — a whisper from the circular orchestra stone carries to the last row of seats.

## The Sacred Quarter

### The Temples
A full **temple for every god of the [[_Armisian Pantheon|Armisian Pantheon]]**, each unique in form and sited with deliberate care: the sea-god faces the harbour, the forge-god is nearest the smithing district, the war-god occupies high ground with a clear view of the walls. No two temples share the same architectural plan. Together they make the sacred quarter look less like a district and more like a collection of arguments.

### The Temple-Tomb of the Anaxos
Near the civic centre: a **massive mortuary complex** in white stone and black obsidian, dedicating to the greatest of the Empire's *Anaxos* — specifically those who conquered **[[Venagillium]]** and **[[An'Hiseret]]**. The entombed lie in sarcophagi beneath the nave; their effigies stand above in painted marble. It is simultaneously a place of worship, of state ceremony, and of warning: the Empire does not forget its greatest, and the greatest are expected to be extraordinary.

---

## The Palace

Not a fortress — the walls make a fortified palace unnecessary — but a **labyrinthine complex** of courts, corridors, store-rooms, and ceremonial chambers spreading across the city's high centre in the Minoan tradition. Multi-storied, unplanned-seeming, sprawling — wings added by successive Anaxos until the whole has the quality of something grown rather than designed. Light-wells drop daylight into the deep interior rooms. Frescoes of the sea, of tribute processions, of the gods run floor-to-ceiling. The storerooms hold hundreds of great **pithoi** — sealed clay jars of oil, grain, and wine, the insurance of empire. Somewhere in the western wing is the throne chamber; finding it the first time without a guide takes most visitors most of a morning.

## Institutions

### The Athenaeum
The **great library and scholarly archive** of the capital — second only to the Athenaeum of **[[Himilkos]]** in scope, but considered by many Armisians to be the more authoritative, housing the official imperial copies of every treaty, census, and legal code since the founding. Attached to it are lecture halls, a scriptorium, and a small astronomical observatory on the roof.

### The Mint
Adjacent to the Bouleuterion: a **heavily guarded, single-entry complex** where all imperial coinage is struck. The *overstrike rooms* — where old, clipped, or foreign coins are melted and recast — are sealed chambers whose workers serve fixed terms and are searched on exit. The Mint Director sits on the suffete's council by right of office.

---

## The Cisterns

Beneath the city, largely invisible to those who live above them: **concentric rings of barrel-vaulted cisterns** carved from the limestone bedrock, fed by aqueduct from hills two days' ride inland. They hold enough water to sustain the city through a year's siege. The access stairwells are narrow, cool even in summer, and permanently echoing with drips. Citizens rarely go down. The cistern-workers are a guild unto themselves, and keep their own calendar of the flood.

---

## The Necropolis

Outside the outer wall, on a low hill facing the sea: a **city of the dead** in miniature. Wealthy families maintain *tholos* tombs — circular beehive-chambers sealed with a single carved lintel stone. Lesser citizens use shaft graves marked with stelae recording name, lineage, and epitaph. The processional road to the necropolis is lined with these markers; it is considered poor form to die without providing one. On the festival of the dead (the *Nekrysia*), the road is walked by all classes together, making it briefly the most democratic place in Armis-Caen.

---

## The Tropaia

Along the main processional avenue from the great gate to the temple quarter: **victory columns**, one per major conquest, each a stone pillar crowned with the captured armour, helm, and weapons of a defeated enemy commander, now slowly rusting into oxide and patina. Generals add a new Tropaion to the avenue when they return in triumph. Reading them from gate to centre is a compressed history of the Empire's military ambition. The oldest is nearly illegible.

## Looking Forward (GM)

A century and a half from now, none of this survives. As the Empire's elites grow deathless on the **[[The Amun-Orb|Amun-Orb]]** and the civil war it sparks tears the Empire apart, the conquered **[[Aldari]]** retake their northern plateau and march on the capital itself — sacking Armis-Caen and ending the line of Anaxos for good (see **[[Timeline (Armis)]]**). The white walls, the Cothon, the Athenaeum's archive: all of it becomes ruin and plunder, while **[[Pattis]]** sails on to declare itself the Empire's sole heir. Foreshadow this only through hubris, never prophecy — the walls should feel eternal to the players right up until they aren't.

## Hooks (GM)

- The **seam between old Armis and old Caen** is where conspiracy, old money, and resentment concentrate. The suffetes of Caen still exist as a title; what authority do they actually hold?
- The **[[Dwarven Clans|Dwarven enclaves]]** settling in the Armis district bring new craft techniques and new wealth — and new suspicion. The first non-human communities inside the capital's walls are a political fact the old families have not finished processing.
- The **Mint** is where economic warfare begins: a sophisticated counterfeiting operation, or the quiet debasement of a rival city's coinage, would have to pass through here — or corrupt it.
- The **Palace's western wing** holds something the last three Anaxos have had sealed. No official record explains why.
- The **cisterns** are a city below the city. Accessible, unmapped in their deeper reaches, and known only to the guild that maintains them.
- The **Necropolis** road, walked by all classes during the Nekrysia, is the one day per year when social hierarchy is suspended — and when certain kinds of conversation can happen between people who would otherwise never meet.
